# A Short History of Artificial Intelligence

This timeline starts at the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project, commonly treated as the event that named and established AI as a research field. The ideas behind AI are older; the early context is included below.

## Before the field was named

- **1943 — McCulloch and Pitts:** A mathematical model of a neuron helped connect logic and computation.
- **1950 — Alan Turing:** “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” asked whether machines can think and proposed what became known as the imitation game (the Turing test).

## From Dartmouth to modern AI

- **1956 — Dartmouth workshop:** John McCarthy and colleagues proposed a summer study of artificial intelligence. The name “artificial intelligence” became the banner for a new research program.
- **1957–1958 — Perceptron:** Frank Rosenblatt developed an early trainable neural-network model, inspiring interest in learning from examples.
- **1959 — Machine learning terminology:** Arthur Samuel popularized the term “machine learning” through work on a checkers-playing program that improved with experience.
- **1960s — Early symbolic programs:** Researchers built systems for language, puzzles, and reasoning. Their success was limited by computing power and the difficulty of representing real-world knowledge.
- **1966 — ELIZA:** Joseph Weizenbaum’s text program demonstrated how simple scripted dialogue could appear conversational; it did not understand language as people do.
- **1970s — First AI winter:** Expectations exceeded available data, computing, and methods, contributing to reductions in funding and interest.
- **1980s — Expert systems and renewed interest:** Rule-based systems captured specialist knowledge for narrow tasks. Their maintenance and brittleness became significant challenges.
- **1986 — Backpropagation popularized for neural networks:** Work by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams helped make multilayer neural-network training practical and influential.
- **Late 1980s–1990s — Second AI winter:** Commercial limits and unmet expectations again reduced enthusiasm for some AI approaches.
- **1997 — Deep Blue:** IBM’s chess system defeated world champion Garry Kasparov in a match, using search and specialized evaluation rather than human-like general reasoning.
- **1997 — LSTM:** Long short-term memory networks introduced a useful approach to learning longer dependencies in sequence data.
- **2000s — Data and compute grow:** The web, larger datasets, GPUs, and improved algorithms made statistical machine learning increasingly effective.
- **2012 — AlexNet:** A deep convolutional neural network achieved a major ImageNet image-recognition result, accelerating interest in deep learning.
- **2014 — Generative adversarial networks:** Ian Goodfellow and collaborators introduced GANs, training a generator and discriminator in competition.
- **2016 — AlphaGo:** DeepMind’s system defeated Go champion Lee Sedol, combining deep neural networks with search.
- **2017 — Transformer:** “Attention Is All You Need” proposed the Transformer, an attention-based architecture that avoids recurrence and became foundational for many language models.
- **2018 — BERT and GPT:** BERT showed the impact of bidirectional pretraining for language understanding; GPT demonstrated generative pretraining followed by adaptation.
- **2020 — GPT-3:** A large language model demonstrated strong few-shot behavior from prompts, while also highlighting limitations such as unreliable outputs and bias.
- **2021 — AlphaFold 2:** DeepMind’s protein-structure predictions showed how machine learning could contribute to scientific research.
- **2022 — ChatGPT:** A conversational interface made large language models widely accessible and brought AI safety, reliability, and societal effects into broader public discussion.
- **2023 onward — Multimodal and tool-using systems:** Models increasingly combine text, images, audio, video, and external tools. Evaluation, privacy, security, cost, and human oversight remain important engineering concerns.

## What the history teaches

1. AI progress comes in waves; impressive demos do not automatically mean reliable products.
2. Data, compute, algorithms, and evaluation all matter—and improvements often arrive together.
3. Different approaches (rules, search, statistics, neural networks) solve different problems; modern systems often combine them.
4. A useful AI engineer measures real task performance, failure modes, cost, safety, and maintainability—not only model size.
